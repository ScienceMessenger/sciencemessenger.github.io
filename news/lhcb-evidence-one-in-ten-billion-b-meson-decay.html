After a 40-year hunt, CERN physicists see first signs of a one-in-ten-billion decay

The upgraded LHCb detector has found evidence, though not yet proof, that a B meson can decay into two muons at the very rare rate the Standard Model predicts.

By Science Messenger Staff · November 3, 2026 · CERN, Switzerland · 4 min read

Physicists at CERN's LHCb experiment have reported the first evidence from a single experiment that a particle called the B0 meson can decay into a pair of muons, heavier cousins of the electron. Theory predicts this happens only about once in every ten billion decays of particles containing a beauty quark, which makes it one of the rarest processes physicists have tried to measure.

The result was announced at a CERN seminar on 3 November 2026. The paper has been posted on the arXiv preprint server and submitted to the journal Physical Review X. It has not yet been peer reviewed.

What was measured

A B0 meson is made of a beauty quark and a down antiquark. A related particle, the Bs meson, decays into two muons about three times per billion decays. That decay was first observed in 2015 in a joint analysis by LHCb and the CMS experiment. The B0 version is predicted to be about 30 times rarer. The 2015 analysis also hinted at the B0 decay, but later measurements with data up to 2018 did not confirm that hint.

The new analysis is one of LHCb's first to use data from the Large Hadron Collider's third run. It relies on proton collisions recorded in 2024 and 2025 with a detector upgraded in 2024 that can record collisions at up to five times its earlier rate. The team used a machine-learning algorithm to separate the roughly 26 B0 decays expected in that data from a large background of unrelated muon pairs.

The 2024 and 2025 data alone gave a signal at 3.0 standard deviations, or sigma. Combined with LHCb data from 2011 to 2018, the significance rose to 3.2 sigma. Three sigma is the usual bar for calling something "evidence", but particle physicists require five sigma to claim a discovery. The measured rate, about 1.1 per ten billion, matches the Standard Model of particle physics, according to Physics World. The same analysis produced the most precise measurements yet of the Bs decay.

Why it matters

Because these decays are so rare and so precisely predicted, they are a sensitive test for unknown particles that could change the rate. This result does not point to new physics. Instead it narrows the room for theories that would push the rate up or down. LHCb spokesperson Tim Gershon of the University of Warwick told Physics World that the measurements rule out models in which these decay rates would be significantly changed.

"The results are very impressive and demonstrate the power of the upgraded LHCb detector" (Robert Fleischer, VU Amsterdam, not involved in the study)

The analysis team was led from the UK, with Harry Cliff of the University of Cambridge among its leaders. Reaching five sigma will require more data. LHCb has planned a further upgrade that would increase its data sample by another factor of ten, although Physics World reports that the UK's funding agency withdrew support for UK work on building it in December 2025.
