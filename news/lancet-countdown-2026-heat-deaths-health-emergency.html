Heat was linked to a record 710,000 deaths in 2023, says the tenth Lancet Countdown report

The annual climate and health report estimates that heat-related deaths have risen sharply since the Paris Agreement, and its authors want climate change recognised as a global health emergency.

By Farlight Staff · October 28, 2026 · University College London, United Kingdom · 4 min read

Every year, a large international team tracks how climate change is affecting human health and how well the world is responding. The tenth edition of this report, the Lancet Countdown on health and climate change, was published in The Lancet on 28 October 2026, ahead of the COP31 climate summit. It was produced by 146 researchers led from University College London, working in collaboration with the World Health Organization.

The report follows 54 indicators, four of them new this year, covering everything from heat and infectious disease to air pollution, food security and money spent on fossil fuels.

Heat deaths at a record

Its headline estimate is that about 710,000 deaths worldwide were linked to heat in 2023, the hottest year in the period studied and the highest toll the team has recorded. The authors attribute 71.5 percent of those deaths to human-caused climate change.

Across 2016 to 2023, heat was linked to an average of about 540,800 deaths a year, around 36 percent more than in the previous decade. The share attributed to climate change rose from 55 percent to about 68 percent. Marina Romanello, the Countdown's executive director at UCL, summed this up as roughly three million heat-related deaths in the eight years since the 2015 Paris Agreement.

"That's roughly equivalent to the entire population of Wales being wiped out in the space of eight years due to heat" (Marina Romanello, executive director, Lancet Countdown, speaking to AFP)

Other warning signs, and some progress

The report also estimates that heat cost about 573 billion working hours and roughly 1.07 trillion US dollars in lost income in 2025, and that dengue reached a record 14.4 million cases in 2024. It found that 71 percent of WHO member states had no plan, as of 2025, for adapting their health systems to climate change.

Not every trend was negative. The authors point to rising investment in clean energy, which AFP reported reached 2.1 trillion US dollars in 2025, about double the amount invested in fossil fuels, and to falling air pollution from fossil fuels in dozens of countries since 2016. But they say progress has been too slow and uneven, and that political attention to climate and health has fallen, with health-and-climate mentions in UN General Debate speeches dropping sharply since 2021.

On that basis the authors call for climate change to be formally recognised as a global health emergency and for a new WHO instrument to coordinate the response. This is a recommendation from the report's authors, not a declaration by WHO.

How to read the numbers

The death figures are modelled estimates rather than a count of individual death certificates, so they carry statistical uncertainty. Romanello told AFP that this year's work was complicated by the disappearance of some data sources, particularly in the United States, where some fossil-fuel subsidy data have not been made public. Most of the experts quoted in coverage are themselves part of the Countdown collaboration, so the report is best read as a large, structured synthesis rather than an independent verdict.
