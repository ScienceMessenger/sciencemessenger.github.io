Eight teeth suggest Homo erectus lived in China's rainforests far later than thought

Fossil teeth from two caves in Guangxi are dated to between roughly 324,000 and 184,000 years ago, and their chemistry points to a life in dense tropical forest.

By Science Messenger Staff · November 18, 2026 · Guangxi, China · 4 min read

Homo erectus, an early human species that appeared about two million years ago, is usually pictured striding across open grasslands. Two papers published in Nature on 18 November suggest that at least some of its last populations in mainland Asia lived in dense tropical rainforest instead, and survived there much later than many researchers had assumed.

The work centres on nine hominin teeth from Baxian Cave and Naxian Cave in Guangxi, southern China. Researchers from the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology in Beijing, Macquarie University and Southern Cross University in Australia, and the Max Planck Institute of Geoanthropology in Germany were among those involved.

Identifying and dating the teeth

The first paper identifies eight of the teeth as Homo erectus, based on the shape of the boundary between enamel and dentine inside the teeth and on ancient proteins preserved in them. The ninth, an upper molar from Baxian Cave, has ambiguous features and remains unassigned; one possibility raised is that it belonged to a Denisovan.

The team used five independent dating methods on the teeth and the surrounding cave deposits. The Baxian teeth date to between about 324,000 and 184,000 years ago, and the Naxian teeth to between about 319,000 and 241,000 years ago. That makes them the youngest securely dated Homo erectus fossils from mainland China. Many researchers had thought the species was gone from mainland Asia by around 400,000 years ago.

A rainforest diet

The second paper looks at chemical signatures in the tooth enamel, along with the teeth of other animals from the caves, which include orangutans and an extinct forest elephant. These signals are consistent with a dense or open-canopy rainforest. Strontium isotopes, which reflect the geology where an individual grew up, suggest that some individuals moved over large distances through the forested limestone landscape, possibly towards what is now northern Vietnam.

"The identification looks really solid." (Richard Potts, Smithsonian Institution's Human Origins Program, not involved in the studies, speaking to National Geographic)

There are limits. The finds are teeth only, with no skulls or skeletons, and the date ranges are wide, spanning well over 100,000 years. The rainforest interpretation rests on chemical proxies from a small number of individuals. The claims about long-distance movement are framed by the researchers as possibilities.

Even so, the results add to growing evidence that Homo erectus could adapt to very different environments. Why the species eventually disappeared, around 115,000 years ago according to current estimates, remains an open question.
