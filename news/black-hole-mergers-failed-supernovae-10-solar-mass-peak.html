Most merging black holes weigh about 10 Suns, and the stars that made them may never have exploded

A Caltech-led look at a decade of gravitational-wave detections finds a crowd of black holes near 10 solar masses and an odd shortage just above them. The pattern fits stars that collapsed quietly instead of going supernova.

By Farlight Staff · November 10, 2026 · California Institute of Technology (Caltech), USA · 4 min read

The textbook ending for a very massive star is a supernova: the core collapses, the outer layers blast away, and a neutron star or black hole is left behind. Theorists have long suspected there is a quieter route too. In a failed supernova, the collapsing core swallows the star almost whole and the explosion never gets going, so a bright star simply winks out and a black hole is left in its place.

A new study in Physical Review D, published on 10 November, argues that gravitational waves may be showing the results of that quieter route on a large scale. The work was led by Katerina Chatziioannou, a physics professor at Caltech, with Isaac Legred, who did the work as a Caltech graduate student and is now a postdoctoral researcher at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, and Jacob Golomb, also a former Caltech PhD student.

A crowd at 10, almost nobody at 14

The LIGO, Virgo and KAGRA detectors pick up ripples in spacetime from pairs of black holes spiralling together and merging, and each signal carries the masses of the two objects. According to Caltech, the team studied 257 such mergers recorded over about ten years. The paper's main analysis uses 153 events from the GWTC-4.0 catalogue, and the authors report that their conclusions hold with the newer GWTC-5.0 catalogue.

Two features stand out. About three-quarters of the black holes in the sample cluster around 10 times the mass of the Sun. Just above that, the numbers collapse: the paper finds the merger rate falls by roughly a factor of 100 between about 10 and 15 solar masses, and for the heavier black hole in each pair it is consistent with zero between about 12 and 16 solar masses, at 90 per cent confidence. Above roughly 16 solar masses the rate picks up again.

"A vast majority of the merging black holes in the universe are about 10 times the mass of our sun." (Katerina Chatziioannou, Caltech, via Phys.org)

The spins point the same way. Caltech says the 10-solar-mass black holes spin within a narrower range than the sample as a whole, which suggests they share a common history rather than being a random mix.

Why a quiet collapse would leave a pile-up

The authors argue that ordinary differences in how compact stellar cores are can explain part of the excess near 10 solar masses, but not a drop this steep. Their proposal is that direct collapse is especially efficient at making black holes in a narrow mass window near 10 solar masses, creating a separate group, possibly set apart from heavier black holes by a gap. The 12-to-16 solar mass shortage was not something the team had predicted, and what causes it remains open.

Failed supernovae themselves have not been confirmed. The best-known candidate, mentioned in Space.com's coverage, is N6946-BH1, a giant star in another galaxy that appears in Hubble Space Telescope images from 2007 but had vanished by 2015, probably after collapsing into a black hole without a visible explosion.

What the study does not show

The result is a statistical inference about a population, not a direct sighting of any star failing to explode. The authors say the data are consistent with a failed-supernova origin for most of these black holes, and Legred has said more research is needed. Other formation stories could still play a part, and the shape of the mass distribution will be tested as LIGO, Virgo and KAGRA keep adding detections.
