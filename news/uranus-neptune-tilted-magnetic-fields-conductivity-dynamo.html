Uranus and Neptune have the strangest magnetic fields in the solar system. Their insides may explain why

Computer models in which the planets' interiors conduct electricity better with depth reproduce the lopsided fields Voyager 2 found. They also suggest those fields may have shifted a lot since the 1980s.

By Farlight Staff · November 3, 2026 · University of Texas at Austin, USA · 4 min read

Only one spacecraft has ever visited Uranus and Neptune. Voyager 2 flew past Uranus in January 1986 and Neptune in August 1989, and its magnetometer found something no one expected. Earth's magnetic field is close to a neat bar magnet, tilted about 11 degrees from the planet's spin axis. Uranus's is tilted about 59 degrees and Neptune's about 47, and both fields are messy and lopsided, with their main magnetic axis passing well away from the planet's centre.

A study published on 3 November in Communications Physics offers an explanation. It was led by Chi Yan, a research associate at the University of Texas Institute for Geophysics in UT Austin's Jackson School of Geosciences, with Krista Soderlund of UT Austin, Jonathan Aurnou and Hao Cao of UCLA, Adam Masters of Imperial College London and Henrik Melin of Northumbria University.

A conductor that changes with depth

Planetary magnetic fields come from a dynamo: electrically conducting fluid churning inside a spinning planet. On Earth that fluid is molten iron in the outer core. Uranus and Neptune, about 14.5 and 17 times Earth's mass, are thought to hold deep layers of water, ammonia and methane under huge pressures and temperatures, around a rocky core, though their exact make-up is uncertain.

Under those conditions, water molecules can break apart into ions, so the material should conduct electricity better the deeper you go. The team built numerical dynamo models with that kind of conductivity profile and tested a range of interior structures. The models produced large-scale, tilted and uneven fields consistent with what Voyager 2 measured, and the paper names the way conductivity changes with depth as a critical ingredient.

Fields that may not sit still

The simulated fields also changed quickly over time, though the pace varied and sometimes slowed for a while. In some model runs the tilt moved by tens of degrees within a few decades; in other stretches it barely shifted.

"In some stretches the tilt swings by tens of degrees within decades." (Chi Yan, University of Texas at Austin, via UT Jackson School of Geosciences)

That matters because more than 30 years have passed since Voyager 2. If the models are right, today's fields could look noticeably different from the ones in the textbooks. Earth's tilt, by comparison, drifts only about a degree every 50 years.

How to check

These are simulations, and each planet's field has been measured up close only once, so how much it has actually changed is unknown. Tom Stallard of Northumbria University, who leads James Webb Space Telescope observations of the planets' faint infrared auroras, says the position of those auroras could reveal how the fields have moved since the 1980s. That work is still under way.

A firm answer will need new spacecraft. Space.com notes that a NASA mission to Uranus has been ranked a top priority, and that China's Tianwen-4 mission, due to launch toward Jupiter in 2029, plans to send a small probe on to Uranus. The authors say any possible change in the fields should be taken into account when planning such missions.
