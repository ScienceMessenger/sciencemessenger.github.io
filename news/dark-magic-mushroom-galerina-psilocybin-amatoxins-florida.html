A tiny brown Florida mushroom makes both a psychedelic and the toxins behind most mushroom deaths

Galerina indica is the first mushroom known to produce psilocybin and amatoxins together. Its genes suggest it borrowed the psychedelic recipe from other fungi.

By Science Messenger Staff · November 11, 2026 · University of Florida, United States · 4 min read

Mushrooms that make psilocybin, the compound behind "magic mushrooms", and mushrooms that make amatoxins, the poisons responsible for most fatal mushroom poisonings, have always been treated as separate groups. A study in Current Biology describes the first species known to make both, and its authors have nicknamed it the dark magic mushroom.

The species is Galerina indica, first described from India in 2015. Researchers led by the University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences found it in Florida wetlands, growing on a moss-covered palm trunk. It is small and brown, roughly the size of a thumbnail, and looks much like many other species. Matthew Smith, the senior author and Florida's state mycologist, says even trained experts struggle to tell such mushrooms apart.

A psychedelic recipe borrowed twice

First author Andrew Kunik, who did the work at the University of Florida, told ScienceAlert that the team cross-checked its results before concluding that both kinds of compound were present. The genus Galerina already includes poisonous species such as the funeral bell, Galerina marginata, which also carries amatoxins.

After sequencing the genome, the team concluded that an ancestor of G. indica could probably already make amatoxins and later picked up the genes for psilocybin through horizontal gene transfer, in which DNA moves between unrelated organisms instead of passing from parent to offspring. A separate Galerina lineage seems to have gained the psilocybin genes in an independent event.

"Two independent horizontal gene transfer events for these specialized metabolites in one mushroom genus are unheard of." (Bryn Dentinger, University of Utah, via Popular Science)

A warning hidden in a blue bruise

Many psilocybin mushrooms stain blue when handled, and some foragers have taken that as a sign a mushroom lacks deadly toxins. G. indica also bruises blue, and the authors write that their finding cautions against reading blue bruising as evidence that amatoxins are absent.

Important gaps remain. The coverage does not report how much of each compound the mushroom contains, so how dangerous it would be to a person is not known. Why a fungus would make both a mind-altering chemical and a lethal one is also unclear; the researchers suggest a mixed chemical defence against animals that eat mushrooms. The specimens are kept at the Natural History Museum of Utah for further testing.

The practical message is simple: nobody should eat wild mushrooms. Small brown mushrooms are notoriously hard to identify, and this one shows that a mushroom with one kind of compound can carry a far more dangerous one as well. Smith says knowing which species grow in a region matters because some can harm people or pets.
