Scientists map 25 million cells in the brain's basal ganglia, a hub tied to Parkinson's and addiction

A package of 26 papers from the NIH-funded BRAIN Initiative Cell Atlas Network builds a cross-species reference map of a deep brain region involved in movement, mood and decision-making.

By Farlight Staff · November 12, 2026 · Allen Institute, Seattle, United States · 4 min read

Deep in the brain sits a cluster of structures called the basal ganglia, which help process movement, mood, motivation and decisions. Problems here are linked to conditions ranging from Parkinson's and Huntington's disease to schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and addiction. Researchers have now published what Nature's news team describes as the most detailed atlas of the region so far.

The work appears as a package of 26 peer-reviewed papers in Cell Press journals, including Cell, Cell Reports, Cell Systems, Cell Genomics and Neuron, published on 12 November 2026. It comes from the BRAIN Initiative Cell Atlas Network (BICAN), a roughly 500-million-dollar effort launched by the US National Institutes of Health in 2022, involving hundreds of scientists at more than 50 institutions in nine countries. The Allen Institute in Seattle, where Hongkui Zeng and Ed Lein helped lead the effort, is among the main contributors.

What is in the map

The atlas profiles 25.5 million cells: 21 million from the brains of six people, 3 million from three macaques and 1.5 million from two marmosets. For each cell the teams measured which genes were active and, in many cases, chemical marks on DNA, electrical activity, how the DNA was folded and where the cell sat in the tissue. Comparing the three primate species, they identified 61 cell types shared across humans, macaques and marmosets.

One study, from Evan Macosko's group at the Broad Institute, examined 1.1 million cells from the striatum, the largest structure in the basal ganglia, taken from 19 deceased donors. Although the striatum has no sharp anatomical borders, the team found six zones with distinct molecular signatures. The differences between zones became less pronounced with age, especially in areas affected by Huntington's and Parkinson's disease.

Clues to disease

Other papers in the package link specific cell types to illness. A study in Cell identified a type of striatal neuron, named STR D2 SMYD2-HTR7, associated with higher genetic risk for schizophrenia. A team at the University of California, San Francisco reported genetic changes tied to autism in particular basal ganglia cell types, and a Cell Reports paper described an improved gene therapy approach that reduced tremor in animal models of Parkinson's disease.

"It's a massive dictionary that we needed in the field" (Ana Muñoz-Manchado, neuroscientist, University of Cádiz, speaking to Nature)

What it does not show

An atlas is a reference tool, not a treatment. The human data come from post-mortem tissue from a limited number of donors, and one study of nearly four million cells from 178 donors found that the cellular make-up of the striatum and prefrontal cortex varied noticeably between people, a reminder that a single map cannot capture everyone. The disease links are associations based on genetics and gene activity, and the Parkinson's gene therapy result was in animals, not people. The data are openly available, and BICAN researchers describe the basal ganglia work as a template for mapping the rest of the brain.
