To recognise life on other worlds, scientists first built an Earth where life never began

A University of Washington-led model runs a lifeless Earth over five billion years and still ends up with oceans and an atmosphere. It gives astronomers a baseline for telling a merely habitable planet from an inhabited one.

By Farlight Staff · November 2, 2026 · University of Washington, USA · 4 min read

How would anyone know that a planet light-years away is alive? Future telescopes will try to read the air of rocky planets around Sun-like stars, but a gas only points to life if ordinary geology and chemistry cannot make it. Answering that means knowing what a planet looks like with no life at all. A team led from the University of Washington has now built that picture for Earth.

The work appeared in The Planetary Science Journal in September 2026 and was publicised by the university on 2 November. It was led by Samantha Gilbert-Janizek, who did it as a UW astronomy doctoral student and is now a postdoctoral fellow at the University of California, Riverside, with UW astronomer Rory Barnes as senior author and co-authors at the Carnegie Institution for Science, the SETI Institute and NASA.

A planet from core to sky

The model links the planet's layers into one system: core, mantle, crust, oceans and atmosphere. It follows how heat, carbon dioxide and water move between them over time while the young Sun slowly brightens. It extends VPLanet, software from Barnes's group whose earlier version handled the interior and starlight but not the surface or the air. The run starts 50 million years after Earth formed and continues to the 5-billion-year mark, about 500 million years beyond today.

As a test, the team compared the simulated planet at an age of 4.5 billion years with 19 measured properties of the real, pre-industrial Earth, including heat flow through the mantle, the strength of the magnetic field, the mass and acidity of the oceans, carbon dioxide and water vapour in the air, and average surface temperature. All 19 fell within the measurement uncertainties. The pre-industrial period was chosen because modern warming has pushed today's climate out of balance.

Oceans without life

The lifeless Earth kept liquid oceans and an atmosphere throughout, and the authors write that life may not be needed to keep a planet habitable over the long term. The university notes they cannot rule out that life on the real Earth helps steady the climate. Their model air is nitrogen with carbon dioxide and water vapour but no oxygen, since Earth's oxygen came from photosynthesis.

That baseline is the point. If the model can reproduce a planet's observed atmosphere without life, that planet probably has no life as we know it. If it cannot, the planet becomes a stronger candidate, although a mismatch alone would not prove anything. Earth's own air shows why: oxygen and methane destroy each other, so finding both suggests something keeps replenishing them.

"This model can help us see how habitable but nonliving planets might mimic living planets." (Samantha Gilbert-Janizek, UC Riverside, via Phys.org)

The team also produced a simulated spectrum of the light the lifeless planet would reflect, covering the wavelengths planned for NASA's proposed Habitable Worlds Observatory. The James Webb Space Telescope and future giant ground-based telescopes are also probing exoplanet atmospheres.

The limits

This is a simplified, one-dimensional computer model, not an observation. It assumes plate tectonics throughout Earth's history, a fixed one-bar nitrogen atmosphere and no loss of gas to space, and it was checked only against pre-industrial Earth, not the poorly known early Earth. Using it for other planets means trusting it beyond the one world it was tuned on, and it does not identify any inhabited exoplanet.
