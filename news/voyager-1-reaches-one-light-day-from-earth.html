Voyager 1 is now so far away that its signals take a full day to reach us

On 18 November, NASA's 49-year-old probe became the first human-made object to reach one light-day from Earth. Only two of its science instruments still work, and its power is running down.

By Science Messenger Staff · November 18, 2026 · NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory, United States · 3 min read

NASA's Voyager 1 spacecraft passed a symbolic mark on 18 November 2026. At 10:16 UTC (5:16 a.m. EST) it reached one light-day from Earth, a distance of about 25.9 billion kilometres (roughly 16 billion miles). No other human-made object has travelled this far.

A light-day is the distance light covers in 24 hours. A radio command sent from Earth now needs a full day to reach the probe, and any reply needs another day to come back. Voyager project scientist Linda Spilker told Space.com that the probe and Earth are exactly 24 hours apart at the speed of light.

Nearly half a century on the road

Voyager 1 launched on 5 September 1977, about two weeks after its twin Voyager 2, on a faster path. It flew past Jupiter and Saturn, where its findings included active volcanoes on Jupiter's moon Io. In 2012 it became the first spacecraft to cross into interstellar space, the region beyond the bubble of particles blown out by the Sun. Voyager 2 followed in 2018.

The probe now travels at about 61,000 kilometres per hour and is roughly 173 times farther from the Sun than Earth is, according to ABC News. NASA's Deep Space Network, with large antennas in Canberra, California and Madrid, still tracks it. Voyager 2 is not expected to reach the one-light-day mark until 2035.

Running low on power

The milestone comes as the mission nears its end. Voyager 1 runs on a nuclear power source whose output falls every year. Engineers have switched off almost all of its equipment, including some heaters, and several of its thrusters are clogging. Spilker told ABC News that power and heat should last until about the early 2030s.

Only two of Voyager 1's science instruments still work. Both measure the magnetic field and the charged gas, or plasma, around the probe, and Spilker expects them to stop within about a year. Voyager 2 still has enough power for three instruments. The spacecraft also briefly went silent last month: NASA lost contact with Voyager 1 for about 48 hours before it came back online on its own, before engineers had sent any troubleshooting commands.

"They are well past the nine lives of your typical cat. It just keeps going." (Linda Spilker, Voyager project scientist, NASA)

Bruce Betts, chief scientist of the Planetary Society, who is not part of the mission team, told ABC News that it is unprecedented for a working spacecraft to last this long. Both Voyagers will mark 50 years in space in late 2027. NASA marked the light-day with a public event, asking people to shine a light at the sky and flash the letters V-G-R in Morse code.
