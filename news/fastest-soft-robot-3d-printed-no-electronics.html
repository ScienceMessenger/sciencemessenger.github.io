A 3D-printed soft robot with no electronics runs at almost 5 metres per second

Engineers at UC San Diego borrowed a trick from the compound steam engine to make an air-powered, all-soft running robot more than 100 times faster than their earlier design. It still has to be tethered to an air supply.

By Science Messenger Staff · November 18, 2026 · University of California San Diego, United States · 4 min read

Soft robots, made from flexible materials rather than metal and motors, are usually slow. A six-legged robot built at the University of California San Diego is a notable exception: it reached a top speed of 4.72 metres per second, which its makers say makes it the fastest robot made entirely of soft materials. The work, by Yichen Zhai and Michael Tolley of UC San Diego's Jacobs School of Engineering, appears in the 18 November 2026 issue of Science Robotics.

The robot is 3D-printed in one piece from a single material and contains no electronics at all. Instead of a computer and motors, it is driven by pressurized air flowing through a network of chambers, valves and tubes printed into its body. That built-in plumbing acts as a fluidic control circuit that tells the legs when to move.

A steam-engine idea, in plastic

The team's earlier walking robot, first built in 2022, managed only about 4 centimetres per second because its air circuits could not handle much pressure or flow. Running faster meant pushing more air, at higher pressure, through larger and stronger internal structures. But simply venting that high-pressure air after one use wastes much of its energy.

To fix that, the researchers copied the logic of the compound steam engine, in which steam expands through more than one chamber in turn. In the new robot, air passes through twice: first through small high-pressure chambers that run the valves, then through larger low-pressure chambers that move the legs. The paper describes this core as a double-expansion pneumatic oscillator that turns a steady supply of high-pressure gas into four alternating pressure signals at high frequency. The authors estimate the robot's mechanical power output at 169 watts.

"Our first walking robot demonstrated what was possible, but it was too slow." (Yichen Zhai, UC San Diego)

In tests the robot ran over concrete, gravel, lawn, mulch and sand, ran down stairs, swam, and kept working underwater. The paper also reports that it could be driven with tap water instead of air as the working fluid.

Limits

The robot is not yet free-roaming. It works only while connected by a tether to a source of compressed air, such as a conventional compressor. The team says future designs will need to use less air so that a portable source, such as a compressed-air cartridge, could power them, and that steering and grasping objects are also still to come.

The researchers suggest that electronics-free robots like this could eventually be useful where electronics fail, for example in areas with strong radiation or magnetic fields, in disaster response or in space. Those uses have not been demonstrated. We did not find independent expert comment on the study at the time of writing. The work was funded by the US National Science Foundation.
