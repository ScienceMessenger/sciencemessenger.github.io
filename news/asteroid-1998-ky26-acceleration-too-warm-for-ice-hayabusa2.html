A tiny asteroid is drifting off course, and the hidden-ice explanation just got harder to believe

Asteroid 1998 KY26 is being nudged by something other than gravity. New thermal simulations suggest it is far too warm to hold the buried ice that would make it a 'dark comet', and Japan has just called off its probe's visit.

By Science Messenger Staff · November 10, 2026 · Georgia Institute of Technology, United States · 4 min read

Asteroid 1998 KY26 is small even by asteroid standards. Observations with the European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope in 2024 showed it is only about 11 metres wide, three to four times smaller than earlier estimates, and that it completes a full turn in roughly five and a half minutes. That makes it one of the fastest-spinning asteroids known.

It also moves in a way gravity alone does not explain. A 2023 analysis of its orbit found a small but statistically significant push, including a component sideways out of the plane of its orbit around the Sun. KY26 was one of six near-Earth objects in that study with this kind of unexplained drift, and the authors floated the idea that they might be "dark comets": bodies that release gas, like a comet, but too faintly to show a visible tail or fuzzy coma.

Running the thermometer

A study by Korbin Hansen and Masatoshi Hirabayashi of the Georgia Institute of Technology, published in the journal Icarus, put that idea to a direct test. The pair built a three-dimensional thermal model of KY26 and tracked how hot its surface and interior get over a full orbit. They ran 13 versions, changing the asteroid's size, how much light it reflects, how well its rock conducts heat and which way its spin axis points.

For water ice to last in the vacuum of space over long periods, it has to stay below about 145 kelvin, or around minus 128 degrees Celsius. In every version of the model, even the coldest parts of KY26 averaged more than 60 degrees warmer than that. No sheltered pocket, on the surface or deep inside, stayed cold enough.

"The water outgassing rate on 1998 KY26 would be too high to maintain buried ice." (Korbin Hansen and Masatoshi Hirabayashi, Georgia Institute of Technology, via ScienceAlert)

Even if ice once made up 1 per cent of the asteroid's mass, the model suggests it would all be gone in about 2,000 years, a blink in the life of an asteroid. The authors conclude a dark comet is highly unlikely. They favour a gentler explanation: the pressure of sunlight hitting the asteroid, combined with the heat its surface radiates back into space, which can add up to a noticeable shove on something this small and light. Earlier measurements of reflected light also point to a bright, rocky body with little water.

No close-up any time soon

The best way to settle the question would have been to go and look. KY26 was the final target of JAXA's Hayabusa2, the probe that brought samples of asteroid Ryugu back to Earth in 2020, with a rendezvous planned for 2031. On 17 November JAXA said it was dropping that plan after a problem with the spacecraft's ion engines was found in August. Hayabusa2 will instead make a fast, close flyby of a different small asteroid, 2015 BM510, in April 2028, with no landing.

A fringe idea has also been aired: Harvard astrophysicist Avi Loeb has proposed that KY26 is a lost Soviet spacecraft. Coverage of that claim notes it relies on an engine burn for which there is no evidence, and it is not supported by other researchers.

The new study has limits. It is a model, built on what little is known about KY26, and the asteroid's past orbit is uncertain, so the authors do not rule out every trace of ice. It also tests one explanation rather than measuring what is actually pushing the asteroid.
