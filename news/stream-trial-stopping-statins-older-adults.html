Can older people stop their statins? A large trial says it is safe in the short term, but not risk free

In a randomised trial of 1,880 people aged 70 and over without heart disease, stopping statins was judged non-inferior at two years, yet heart attacks, strokes and deaths were more common in those who stopped.

By Science Messenger Staff · November 7, 2026 · University of Bern, Switzerland · 4 min read

Many older adults take a statin for years to lower the chance of a first heart attack or stroke, often alongside a long list of other medicines. Whether they can safely stop has been an open question, because few trials have enrolled people in their late seventies with several chronic illnesses. A new randomised trial called STREAM now offers some of the first direct evidence.

The study, led by Nicolas Rodondi of the Institute of Primary Health Care at the University of Bern, was published in the New England Journal of Medicine and presented at the American Heart Association Scientific Sessions in Chicago. It was funded by the Swiss National Science Foundation.

Who took part

The trial enrolled 1,880 people aged 70 or older at 24 centres in Switzerland, France and the Netherlands. Their average age was 77.5, just under half were women, and about a third had diabetes. All had at least two chronic conditions, had used a statin for at least a year to prevent a first cardiovascular event, and had no known heart or blood vessel disease. Around 70 percent had been taking a statin for more than six years.

Participants were randomly assigned either to keep taking their statin or to stop it along with any other cholesterol-lowering drug. The main measure was the first occurrence of a non-fatal heart attack, a non-fatal ischaemic stroke or death from any cause.

What the trial found

At two years, the stopping strategy met the trial's pre-set test of non-inferiority. People who stopped spent an average of 96.5 weeks free of a major event, compared with 98.5 weeks for those who continued, a gap of two weeks that fell inside the allowed margin of 5.2 weeks. Rodondi estimated that for every 40 people who stopped, there was one additional major cardiovascular event or death over two years.

Over the full follow-up of up to four years (median 103 weeks), the difference grew clearer. A primary-outcome event occurred in 12.7 percent of people who stopped versus 9.2 percent of those who continued. Heart attacks occurred in 1.6 versus 0.6 percent, ischaemic strokes in 2.2 versus 0.8 percent and deaths in 9.8 versus 8.0 percent. Serious adverse events were reported in 43 percent of the stopping group and 39.8 percent of the continuing group.

The hoped-for benefits of stopping did not clearly appear. The researchers found no meaningful differences between groups in quality of life, pain, falls, risk of muscle loss or adherence to other medicines. In June 2026 the trial's independent safety monitoring board advised that people in the stopping group discuss restarting statins with their doctors.

Limits and outside views

STREAM was open label, so participants and doctors knew who had stopped, and the results apply only to people without known cardiovascular disease. The authors note that changes in other preventive medicines during follow-up could have influenced the results, and they call for more trials of stopping specific drugs. Rodondi said the small rise in risk means no general recommendation to stop statins can be made for this group.

"We should certainly not rush to stop statins based on this, but we should talk with our patients about their priorities." (Karen Alexander, Duke Clinical Research Institute, discussant at the AHA meeting)

Others were more cautious still. Dinesh Kalra of the University of Louisville, speaking in a personal capacity, told TCTMD that people who tolerate statins should keep taking them because those who stop will have more heart attacks and strokes over time. For now, the trial gives doctors and older patients numbers to weigh together rather than a simple rule.
