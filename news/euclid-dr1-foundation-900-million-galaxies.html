Euclid's first big data haul maps 900 million galaxies, and it covers only 14% of the job

ESA's dark-universe telescope has opened 13 months of survey data, including about 15,000 possible gravitational lenses. The measurements needed for cosmology arrive in 2027.

By Farlight Staff · November 12, 2026 · European Space Agency (ESAC), Spain · 4 min read

Euclid, the European Space Agency telescope launched in July 2023 to study dark matter and dark energy, released its first large batch of survey data on 12 November. Called DR1-Foundation, it covers about 13 months of observations, from spring 2024 to spring 2025, and serves as a preview: the complete first data release is due in mid-2027.

The release spans roughly 1,900 square degrees of sky, an area equal to about 9,500 full Moons, and takes in light from more than a billion objects. Among them are more than 900 million galaxies, over 60 million candidate quasars (the bright cores of galaxies whose central black holes are feeding), and spectra for more than 160 million sources, which help astronomers pin down distances and chemical makeup. The whole package comes to about 1.7 petabytes, built from around 10,000 science images taken by Euclid's visible-light camera, VIS, and its near-infrared instrument, NISP.

For comparison, Euclid's earlier public data covered just a few days of observations over about 60 square degrees.

"These are far more than beautiful pictures; today marks a scientific milestone in our study of the cosmos." (Carole Mundell, ESA Director of Science, via Phys.org)

A record number of lenses

When a massive galaxy sits in front of a more distant one, its gravity bends and magnifies the light from behind, sometimes into arcs or complete rings. These strong gravitational lenses let astronomers weigh the foreground galaxy, dark matter included, and spot faint galaxies that would otherwise be out of reach. DR1-Foundation contains about 15,000 lens candidates, found with a mix of machine learning, visual checks and help from citizen scientists. That is more than all earlier lens catalogues put together.

"Before Euclid, we had fewer than 1,000 confirmed strong lenses. Now we have 15,000 new candidates." (Hrishabh Srivastava, University of British Columbia, via Phys.org)

Over its planned six-year survey, Euclid is expected to turn up more than 100,000 strong lenses.

Why the map matters

Dark matter and dark energy together account for about 95% of the universe's matter and energy, yet neither has been directly detected. Euclid's plan is to chart billions of galaxies across roughly a third of the sky, measuring how their shapes are slightly warped by matter along the line of sight (weak lensing) and how they cluster. Together with distances, that builds a 3D map of how the cosmic web grew over time, which is where the effects of the dark components should show up.

Not cosmology yet

This release covers only about 14% of the area the full survey will map, and the lens candidates still need follow-up observations to confirm them. The higher-level weak-lensing and galaxy-clustering products, which the first dark energy measurements will depend on, are not part of it; ESA says they will be published in mid-2027 to complete Data Release 1, with cosmology results to follow. More than 2,500 researchers at over 300 institutions in the Euclid Consortium are already working with the data.
