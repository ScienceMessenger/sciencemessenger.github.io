His teeth show a Stone Age Danish fisher's diet changing as the first farmers arrived

Rødhals Man grew up on seafood around 6,000 years ago. From his teens, his meals shifted toward land foods that were probably grown or raised by newly arrived farmers.

By Science Messenger Staff · November 19, 2026 · University of Gothenburg, Sweden · 3 min read

Around 3900 BC, farming reached Zealand, the largest island in Denmark. For thousands of years before that, people in the region had lived by fishing, hunting and gathering. A study published in the journal Antiquity on 16 November 2026 looks at one man who seems to have lived through the changeover.

He is known as Rødhals Man, after the shell midden, a mound of discarded shells, on the small island of Sejerø where his skeleton was dug up in 1956. A team of 17 researchers led by Anders Fischer of the University of Gothenburg and John Meadows of the Leibniz Centre for Archaeology in Germany re-examined the bones and the excavation records.

A life written in layers of tooth

Radiocarbon dates, adjusted for the distorting effect of a seafood diet, place his death most likely between about 3890 and 3770 BC. He was about 1.55 metres tall and died between the ages of 30 and 40. His ancient DNA is entirely that of the region's Western Hunter-Gatherers, with no farmer ancestry, and it suggests dark skin and an estimated 85% probability of blue eyes.

To track his meals over time, the team sampled thin, successive layers of dentine inside his teeth, which build up through childhood and adolescence, and measured their chemical isotopes. As a child he ate mostly marine food from shallow coastal waters. From around age 11, more open-sea fish appear. From adolescence onward, the share of protein from land sources rose sharply, which is unusual for coastal foragers of his culture. Because he did not eat freshwater fish, as inland hunters did, the authors argue that the extra land food probably came from farmers.

His grave points the same way. He lay on his back inside a frame of stones, with what may be an archer's wrist guard, a burial style closer to those of early farmers than to typical hunter-gatherer graves of the time. His arm bones show signs of heavy muscle use, possibly from years of paddling.

One man, one generation

The researchers are careful about how much one skeleton can say. Bone chemistry alone cannot tell whether he farmed, traded with farmers, or simply hunted more on land. It is also unclear whether he lived mainly on Sejerø or on a bigger island, and the 1956 dig was rushed, so small objects in the grave may have been missed.

"It is only one person's story, and only one generation." (Anders Fischer, University of Gothenburg)

Still, the find is rare. Hunter-gatherers seem to vanish from the archaeological record in this region almost as soon as farmers appear, which has left open how the two groups met. Rødhals Man suggests that at least some foragers took up parts of the newcomers' way of life while keeping their own ancestry and much of their seafood diet. The team reports a similar pattern in two slightly later individuals from Evensås in western Sweden.
