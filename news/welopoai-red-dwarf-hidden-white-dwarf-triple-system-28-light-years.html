Astronomers went looking for planets 28 light-years away and found a dead star instead

A wobbling red dwarf turned out to be circling a hidden white dwarf, making its system the closest known trio with two white dwarfs. The researchers say they cannot yet explain how it formed.

By Farlight Staff · November 18, 2026 · Trottier Institute for Research on Exoplanets, Université de Montréal, Canada · 4 min read

A team from the Université de Montréal was measuring a faint red dwarf star for the tiny back-and-forth wobble that an orbiting planet would cause. What they got was a wobble far too big for any planet, and a star system that its own discoverers say they cannot fully account for.

"We were actually looking for planets." (Étienne Artigau, Trottier Institute for Research on Exoplanets, Université de Montréal, via Phys.org)

The star, catalogued as IRAS 21500+5903, sits about 28 light-years away. It now has a Hawaiian name, Welopōʻai, chosen through a naming programme run by the ʻImiloa Astronomy Center of Hawaiʻi. The name is usually translated as "path of family traits". The study, led by Artigau, is published in Astronomy & Astrophysics.

Too heavy, too dim

The team tracked the red dwarf for almost five years with SPIRou, a spectrograph on the Canada-France-Hawaii Telescope on Maunakea. Its speed toward and away from Earth swung by about 16 kilometres per second from one extreme to the other, roughly a thousand times more than the researchers expected from a planet.

Whatever was pulling on the star had to weigh more than half as much as the Sun, yet it gave off almost no light. A neutron star or black hole was considered and set aside; a cool white dwarf, the dense leftover core of a dead star, fit best. Speckle imaging with the ʻAlopeke instrument on the Gemini North telescope, also on Maunakea, then picked out the faint companion directly, less than an arcsecond from the red dwarf.

Combining the star's motion with the images gave precise numbers. The red dwarf has about 42 per cent of the Sun's mass. The white dwarf has about 62 per cent, measured to within 0.7 per cent, which the authors say makes it the most precise mass of this kind for any white dwarf within about 65 light-years. The two circle each other every 9.8 years at a distance of about 4.6 times the Earth-Sun distance, a bit less than Jupiter's distance from the Sun. A second white dwarf, already known, orbits the pair from roughly 124 times the Earth-Sun distance.

Why it is a puzzle

Before it died, the inner white dwarf was a star about 40 per cent more massive than the Sun. Late in life it would have swollen into a giant hundreds of times its original size, big enough to engulf the space where the red dwarf now orbits. Stars caught inside a giant like that are expected to spiral inward and end up in very tight orbits, or merge. Instead, the red dwarf sits on a wide, nearly circular path.

"This system should not exist." (Patrick Dufour, Université de Montréal, via Phys.org)

There are other oddities. The white dwarfs point to an old system, while the red dwarf looks younger, and the infrared data show a tentative hint of dust around the inner white dwarf. The authors say this is the closest known triple system containing more than one white dwarf and that it offers a rare test of how stars behave when one engulfs another.

The explanation is still open. The team plans to keep watching the system and to model how the three stars' gravity could have shaped its history, and the dust signal has yet to be confirmed.
