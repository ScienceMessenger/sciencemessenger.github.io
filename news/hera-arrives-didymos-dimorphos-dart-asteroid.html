Europe's Hera probe has reached the asteroid pair NASA deliberately rammed in 2022

ESA's first planetary defence spacecraft has sent back its first close-up of Didymos, the bigger partner of the moonlet DART struck. The real crash-site investigation starts now.

By Science Messenger Staff · November 5, 2026 · European Space Agency (ESOC), Germany · 4 min read

In September 2022, NASA flew a spacecraft straight into a small asteroid on purpose to see whether a collision could push it off course. Four years later, a European probe has arrived to inspect the scene. ESA's Hera spacecraft reached the Didymos asteroid system in November 2026, about a month ahead of its original schedule, and ESA released its first close-up image on 5 November.

Didymos is roughly 780 metres across. A much smaller moonlet, Dimorphos, about 150 metres wide, circles it at a distance of around a kilometre. Neither poses any threat to Earth, which is why the pair was chosen for the test.

What DART changed

NASA's Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) spacecraft, which weighed about 580 kilograms, hit Dimorphos on 26 September 2022 at roughly 6 kilometres per second. The impact cut the time Dimorphos takes to orbit Didymos by about 33 minutes, a much bigger change than scientists had expected. It was the first time people had measurably changed the path of a natural object in the Solar System.

What DART could not reveal was the full aftermath: the size of the crater, how much rock was blasted away, and the masses and internal makeup of the two asteroids. Those details matter for working out how hard you would need to hit a more dangerous asteroid to nudge it aside. Hera's job is to fill those gaps.

A side nobody had seen

Hera launched on 7 October 2024 and used a flyby of Mars in March 2025 to bend its path toward Didymos. Its first close-up, taken with its black-and-white Asteroid Framing Camera, shows a world covered in boulders and craters, with a ridge around its equator and patterns of grooves. Dimorphos sits mostly in shadow, so the DART impact site is not yet visible.

"This is the other side of Didymos from the one that DART's camera showed us shortly before impact." (Patrick Michel, Hera principal investigator, via Popular Science)

One open question is whether debris thrown off Dimorphos crossed the short gap and landed on Didymos, adding to the features in the new picture. ESA mission scientist Michael Kueppers said the team has exciting work ahead to sort that out.

These are first-look images, not scientific results. Over the coming months Hera is due to map both asteroids in detail, and it carries two suitcase-sized CubeSats, Milani and Juventas, for even closer looks. ESA has said sharper images of Didymos and Dimorphos will follow on 25 November.
