Earth has had a second moon since spring, and it is only about the size of a person

A tiny asteroid called 2026 QQ8 was pulled into a loose loop around Earth in April. Astronomers confirmed it is a natural rock, not space junk, and it is due to slip away within weeks.

By Farlight Staff · October 29, 2026 · IAU Minor Planet Center, United States · 4 min read

On the night of 19 to 20 August, Hungarian astronomer Krisztián Sárneczky picked up a faint moving dot in images from the Piszkéstető mountain station in Hungary. Follow-up tracking showed it was not passing Earth on its own path around the Sun. It was going around Earth.

The object, now designated 2026 QQ8, is a temporary mini-moon: a small asteroid briefly held by Earth's gravity before the Sun's pull takes it away again. The International Astronomical Union's Minor Planet Center announced it on 29 October in an electronic circular titled "Temporarily Captured Object".

"Orbit integrations indicate that this object is temporarily bound to the Earth." (Minor Planet Center, MPEC 2026-U95)

Rock or rubbish?

Small things circling Earth are often spent rocket stages, so the first question was whether 2026 QQ8 is natural. In early September even orbit-watchers were unsure. The Minor Planet Center's circular settled it as far as current data allow: its orbit fit, which includes the small push from sunlight, found no link to any known artificial object, and it is listed as a natural asteroid.

It is very small. Its brightness puts it at roughly 1 to 3 metres across, depending on how reflective its surface is; Live Science, citing the IAU, gives about 2 metres. A rock that size poses no danger. If it ever did hit the atmosphere it would most likely break up and burn as a bright fireball.

A loose, short-lived orbit

Orbit calculations from NASA's JPL suggest 2026 QQ8 drifted into Earth's neighbourhood in April and passed about 1.2 million kilometres from Earth on 1 May before being caught. Its closest pass so far came on 18 August, at roughly 537,000 kilometres, about 1.4 times the Moon's distance, the day before it was spotted.

The stay will be brief. JPL's orbit shows one more pass on 13 December at around 1.35 million kilometres, after which it should break free. Coverage differs slightly on timing, from mid-December to January 2027. Around the Sun, the asteroid takes 359 days per lap, almost exactly a year, so it keeps returning near Earth about once a decade. The next close approach in the current orbit solution is in 2058.

Not the first, and not the last

Earth has caught visitors like this before. 2006 RH120, a few metres wide, circled for about a year in 2006 and 2007. 2020 CD3, discovered in February 2020, had been bound for more than a year before it left that spring. 2024 PT5, about 10 metres across, stayed for roughly two months in late 2024, and its colour suggests it may be a chip knocked off the Moon. Researchers think Earth holds at least one tiny mini-moon at most times; most are simply too small and faint to find.

Its size is an estimate, since nobody has measured it directly, and its exit date depends on an orbit based on about three months of observations. New survey telescopes such as the Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile are expected to catch many more of these brief companions.
