Just 403 giant data centres may use nearly as much electricity as an entire US state

A Harvard study estimates that America's largest data centres drew up to 99 terawatt-hours in a year, more than half of it generated from gas and coal.

By Science Messenger Staff · November 16, 2026 · Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, United States · 4 min read

The cloud is not weightless. A study published on 16 November in Nature Sustainability puts a number on how much power the biggest American data centres, the so-called hyperscale facilities, actually use, and where that power comes from.

Researchers at the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, led by postdoctoral fellow Gianluca Guidi with corresponding author Francesca Dominici, found 403 hyperscale centres across the US using a commercial database and satellite imagery. They then estimated each building's yearly electricity use from its size and likely operating hours, for the period May 2024 to April 2025.

A state-sized appetite

Together the 403 facilities used an estimated 68 to 99 terawatt-hours, depending on how hard they were assumed to run. That is about 1.8% of all electricity consumed in the US. The upper estimate comes close to the yearly use of a whole state such as Indiana, Michigan or Tennessee.

"It's pretty extraordinary—a few hundred buildings are consuming as much as an entire state." (Gianluca Guidi, Harvard, to National Geographic)

Using Environmental Protection Agency data on the power mix serving each facility, the team estimated that about 54% of the electricity came from fossil fuels: 38% from natural gas and 15% from coal. Nuclear supplied around a fifth. That works out to roughly 25.7 million tonnes of carbon dioxide a year, two to three times an earlier estimate based on 2018 data. About a quarter of the power came from grid regions dirtier than the national average.

Concentrated, and still growing

The demand is clustered. Virginia, Oregon, Ohio and Iowa together accounted for more than half of the electricity used by the facilities studied. Hundreds more hyperscale centres are planned or under construction.

The authors stress that their numbers are estimates, not metered readings. Data were not available for every hyperscale site, and the method cannot separate ordinary cloud computing from AI workloads. Because the building boom has continued since April 2025, Guidi expects today's figures to be higher. The study also did not measure health effects from the extra power-plant pollution; Dominici says that is the team's next step.

Kasparas Spokas of the Clean Air Task Force, who was not involved, told National Geographic the findings should be a wake-up call, since the way new data-centre demand is met will shape power-sector emissions for decades.
