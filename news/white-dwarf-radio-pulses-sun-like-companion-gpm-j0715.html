A dead star is beaming radio pulses every eight minutes, and its partner looks a lot like our Sun

Australian astronomers traced a repeating radio signal to a white dwarf circling a Sun-like star. The pulses are clean enough to follow the dead star around its orbit, and they even picked up a storm on its companion.

By Farlight Staff · November 12, 2026 · ICRAR / Curtin University, Australia · 4 min read

Since 2021, radio astronomers have been finding a strange kind of signal: sources that switch on and off on a cycle of minutes to hours, much slower than ordinary pulsars. About a dozen of these long-period transients are now known, and what powers them is still debated. A study published on 12 November in Nature Astronomy pins one of them on a pairing nobody had seen before: a white dwarf orbiting a star much like the Sun.

The source is called GPM J071507−114153. It flashes about every 7.9 minutes, which the team takes to be the spin of the white dwarf, the Earth-sized leftover core of a dead star. The research was led by Sam McSweeney, who did the work at the Curtin University node of the International Centre for Radio Astronomy Research (ICRAR) and is now a commissioning scientist at the SKA Observatory, with Natasha Hurley-Walker and colleagues.

From a sky survey to one faint star

The signal turned up in a survey of the Milky Way made with the Murchison Widefield Array (MWA) in Western Australia. Doctoral student Csanád Horváth developed the search method that flagged it, and the Pawsey Supercomputing Research Centre processed more than 100 hours of observations. The MeerKAT radio telescope in South Africa then narrowed the position to a tiny patch of sky containing a single faint star. Spectra from the Keck I telescope in Hawaii showed it to be a G-type star, with a temperature and mass close to the Sun's.

That was a surprise. Earlier long-period transients with a white dwarf involved small, cool red dwarfs, and in those systems it was unclear which star made the pulses. Here the arrival times of the pulses shift by up to a few seconds as the white dwarf swings around its partner, which places the source at or near the white dwarf.

"This is the first time the pulses are clear enough to see the white dwarf moving in its orbit around its companion." (Sam McSweeney, SKA Observatory, via Space.com)

A storm seen through radio light

The pulses are also modulated on a cycle of about 1.3 days, which the team reads as the orbital period of the two stars. They are visible for roughly six hours of each orbit and then fade. As the white dwarf passed behind its companion, the polarisation of its pulses changed quickly, which the authors link to the radio waves crossing the Sun-like star's hot outer atmosphere. The team says one episode was most likely a prominence, a huge loop of magnetised gas thrown up from the star's surface, similar to the flares on the Sun that drive auroras on Earth.

The researchers place the system more than 5,000 light-years away. Some coverage gave a figure closer to 10,000 light-years; the paper's own distance estimate was not available to us, so the exact value should be treated as uncertain. Timing also suggests the Sun-like star is not overflowing onto the white dwarf, so the radio beam seems to need some other kind of interaction between the two.

One system cannot settle what powers all long-period transients, and the authors note the class may contain more than one type of object. The MWA and MeerKAT are forerunners of the Square Kilometre Array telescopes being built in Australia and South Africa, which the team expects to find many more.
