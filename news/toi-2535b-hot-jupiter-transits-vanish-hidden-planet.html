A giant planet stopped crossing its star while TESS was watching, and an unseen neighbour is to blame

For seven years NASA's TESS saw the hot Jupiter TOI-2535 b pass in front of its star. Then the crossings shrank and stopped. Astronomers say the pull of a second, hidden giant planet has tipped its orbit out of line with Earth.

By Science Messenger Staff · November 9, 2026 · University of California, Los Angeles, United States · 4 min read

Most of the planets known beyond the Solar System were found because they pass in front of their star and dim it slightly once per orbit. That trick depends on luck: the orbit has to be lined up almost edge-on to Earth. A study published on 9 November in The Astrophysical Journal Letters describes the first confirmed planet around a single star whose crossings have been seen to fade out and stop altogether.

The planet is TOI-2535 b, a hot Jupiter with roughly the mass of Jupiter that circles its star every 8.24 days, far closer in than Mercury sits to the Sun. Its host is an old orange dwarf, a little less massive than the Sun and about 9.7 billion years old, roughly 1,100 light-years from Earth. The work was led by Samuel Yee, a 51 Pegasi b Fellow at UCLA, with Erik Petigura and an international team.

Late, early, shallower, gone

NASA's Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) looked at the system in five stretches between April 2019 and March 2026. Almost nothing about the planet's crossings stayed constant. They arrived up to about six hours earlier or later than a steady orbit would predict, one of the largest timing swings known for a hot Jupiter. Each crossing also got shorter and shallower, a sign that the planet's path was sliding toward the edge of the star's disc. In the last TESS look, in early 2026, no crossing showed up at all.

Ground-based telescopes in the Las Cumbres Observatory network and at several smaller observatories caught extra crossings along the way. The NEID spectrograph on the WIYN telescope at Kitt Peak, Arizona, measured the star's wobble to confirm that the object is a planet and not a second star.

"The planet's orbit is tilting so quickly that its transit disappeared while we were watching." (Samuel Yee, UCLA, via Space.com)

A hidden partner doing the tilting

The planet is not gone, and nothing went wrong with the telescope. Its orbit is slowly swivelling, like a spinning top that wobbles. When the team modelled the timing and shape changes together, the best explanation was a second planet nearby, orbiting on a plane tilted at least about 5 degrees from the first. Their gravity makes both orbits precess, with one full cycle taking roughly 30 to 50 years. That means TOI-2535 b should start crossing its star again within a few decades.

The hidden neighbour has not been seen directly. Its existence is inferred from its gravitational effects. The models suggest it is at least as massive as Saturn and probably locked in a regular rhythm with the hot Jupiter, most likely completing two orbits for every one of TOI-2535 b's, or three for every two. The exact mass, period and tilt are not yet pinned down, and the leftover scatter in the data hints that more than one unseen planet could be involved. The authors say a dense campaign of star-wobble measurements is now the best way to find it.

The system also matters for how hot Jupiters end up so close to their stars. A giant planet with a close giant neighbour probably did not get flung inward on a wild, stretched orbit. It more likely drifted in slowly through the disc of gas it formed in. Such discs tend to leave planets in flat systems, though, so something must have tilted these two orbits later, and what did it is still an open question.
