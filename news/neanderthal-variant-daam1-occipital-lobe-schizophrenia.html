Four missing letters of Neanderthal DNA are tied to a smaller visual brain region and lower schizophrenia risk

A variant many people outside Africa still carry sits next to a brain-development gene. Its carriers have slightly smaller occipital lobes and somewhat lower odds of schizophrenia, though nobody yet knows why.

By Science Messenger Staff · November 16, 2026 · Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology, Germany · 4 min read

Neanderthal skulls were longer and less rounded than ours, which has long hinted that the proportions of their brains differed from modern human brains. A study published on 16 November in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS) points to one small piece of Neanderthal DNA that still seems to nudge brain proportions in living people.

The authors are Hugo Zeberg, who works at the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology in Leipzig and the Karolinska Institutet in Stockholm, and Svante Pääbo of the Max Planck institute, who won the 2022 Nobel Prize in medicine for sequencing ancient genomes.

A tiny deletion with a long history

The variant is a deletion of four DNA letters on chromosome 14, close to a gene called DAAM1 that is involved in building the brain. According to ScienceAlert, all five Neanderthal genomes the pair examined carry the deletion, while the two Denisovan genomes do not. The Neanderthal-like stretch of DNA around it is nearly 60,000 letters long, a sign that it arrived through interbreeding rather than being inherited from an older shared ancestor. Today it turns up only in people whose ancestry lies outside sub-Saharan Africa.

Carriers show altered DAAM1 activity. Using genetic and brain data from the UK Biobank, the researchers also found that they have an occipital lobe, the part at the back of the brain that handles vision, about 0.85 cubic centimetres smaller for each copy of the variant. In data from the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium, each copy went with roughly 5% lower odds of schizophrenia, and people with two copies had odds almost 10% lower. Neanderthal ancestry as a whole showed no such pattern.

What it does not show

These are statistical associations across large populations, not proof that the variant protects anyone, and the 10% figure is a relative change in odds, not a 10-point drop in an individual's risk. Scientific American reports no link with depression, bipolar disorder or ADHD. Any connection with schooling was tiny or absent: at most about two weeks of education per copy, against an average of around 15 years. The authors write that the results caution against equating the size of a brain region with cognitive ability.

"Whether the reduced schizophrenia risk is directly related to the smaller occipital lobe remains, at least for us, unknown." (Hugo Zeberg, speaking to ScienceAlert)

There is also a puzzle. Fossil skulls suggest Neanderthals had relatively large occipital regions, so a Neanderthal variant that shrinks this area in modern people is not what one might expect. Zeberg told ScienceAlert that a single variant need not reflect Neanderthal anatomy overall.

Outside researchers were broadly positive but cautious. Nadav Ahituv of the University of California, San Francisco, who reviewed the paper for PNAS, described it to Scientific American as linking an inherited Neanderthal variant to anatomical differences in people today. Speaking to Spain's Science Media Centre, archaeologist Javier Baena of the Autonomous University of Madrid called the work rigorous but said he doubted the schizophrenia effect was statistically meaningful. How the deletion changes DAAM1, and how that might affect the brain, remains to be worked out.
