An AI that had never seen a black hole was asked to image M87*. It drew the same ring

Astronomers at CSIRO rebuilt the famous Event Horizon Telescope picture from public data, using a method that avoids calibration errors and assumes nothing about black holes. The ring came back the same size.

By Farlight Staff · November 11, 2026 · CSIRO, Australia · 4 min read

In April 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) collaboration showed the world its first picture of a black hole: a lopsided ring of light around M87*, the giant black hole at the heart of the galaxy Messier 87, some 55 million light-years away. A small team at CSIRO, Australia's national science agency, has now rebuilt that picture from the same public data with a method of its own, and the ring is still there. The work was published in Nature on 11 November.

Why check at all? The EHT links radio dishes across the planet into one Earth-sized telescope, but it only samples the sky at scattered points, so any picture has to be reconstructed from incomplete information. The original image came from four separate imaging teams. All four found a ring, yet they disagreed on finer features, and a few outside reanalyses went further and questioned whether the ring was real or a side effect of processing choices.

Triplets instead of pairs

The CSIRO approach, led by astronomer Samuel Lai with project leader Nithyanandan Thyagarajan, tackles two weak spots. First, instead of working with pairs of telescopes, it combines measurements from three or more at a time into quantities called closure invariants. In those combinations, many errors added by Earth's atmosphere and by individual telescopes cancel out, so the result does not hinge on how each dish was calibrated.

Second, the images are produced by a machine-learning tool the team built, called GenDIReCT. It was trained on everyday pictures such as cats, dogs and cars rather than on simulated black holes, so it had no built-in expectation of a ring. The team tested it on shapes that are not rings and on real data from another galaxy, Centaurus A, and froze its settings before running it on M87*, to keep their own expectations out of the result.

"This is the first truly independent validation of the EHT image, an important step in the scientific process." (Samuel Lai, CSIRO, via Phys.org)

Same ring, three different years

The team used the publicly released EHT measurements from 2017, 2018 and 2021, eight observing nights in all. Every dataset produced a compact ring. On the best night of each year the ring had a consistent size and orientation, and the team measured its diameter at 42.2 millionths of an arcsecond, give or take 1.5. That fits a black hole of about 6.5 billion times the Sun's mass, seen roughly 20 degrees from the direction of its jet, in line with the EHT's own results. A more traditional statistical imaging method, run on the same triplet-based data, backed up the shape.

The ring's orientation also shifted between the 2017 and 2018 observations, which the authors attribute to the turbulent flow of gas around the black hole. Some of the change seen within 2017 itself may instead come from the patchier coverage on two of that year's nights.

"Independent validation is at the heart of the scientific process." (Laurent Loinard, director of the EHT Collaboration, via Space.com)

What it doesn't settle

The agreement is about the overall shape. The new images differ from the EHT's in finer details, such as the thickness of the ring and how its brightness varies around the circle, and on a few lower-quality nights the reconstructions were less clean. Because the study reuses the EHT's own observations, it checks how the image was made rather than offering a new look at the black hole. Thyagarajan says the triplet technique might one day help in other fields, such as medical imaging or earthquake mapping, but that has not been tried yet.
