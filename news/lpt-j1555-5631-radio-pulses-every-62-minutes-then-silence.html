Something in the Milky Way pulsed every 62 minutes, then went quiet

Radio telescopes caught an object flashing twice per cycle, with each flash twisted in the opposite direction. It has not been seen since 2022, and astronomers still cannot say whether it is a white dwarf or a neutron star.

By Science Messenger Staff · November 6, 2026 · Sweet Briar College, USA · 4 min read

Most pulsars, the spinning remains of dead stars, flash many times a second. A small group of objects found over the past few years does the same trick on a scale of minutes or hours, and nobody is sure what they are. A newly reported member of that group was caught during a single burst of activity in May 2022 and has not been detected since.

Astronomers call these objects long-period radio transients. The newest one, named LPT J1555−5631, is described in The Astrophysical Journal by a team led by Scott Hyman of Sweet Briar College in Virginia, with colleagues from Curtin University in Australia, the US National Radio Astronomy Observatory and the US Naval Research Laboratory.

Found by accident

Hyman was searching archived data from South Africa's MeerKAT radio telescope, taken for a survey of supernova remnants, when he spotted a source that flashed like a pulsar but many minutes apart. Australia's ASKAP telescope happened to have watched the same patch of sky for about 10 hours on the same day, 14 May 2022. Together the two datasets cover about 15 hours and pin the period at 62.2 minutes. Earlier and later observations, going back and forward about five years, show no pulses, which means the active spell lasted somewhere between 15 hours and 10 months.

Each cycle held two flashes of similar brightness. The wider main pulse was a mix of linearly and circularly polarised radio waves. The narrower second pulse was almost entirely circularly polarised, and its circular polarisation always had the opposite sign, or handedness, to the main pulse's.

Two poles, slightly out of line

That pattern is familiar from ordinary pulsars. When a star's magnetic axis lies almost at right angles to its spin axis, Earth sees a beam from one magnetic pole and then from the other on every turn, and the magnetic field points toward us at one pole and away at the other. The team classes the object as this kind of near-orthogonal rotator.

In a simple magnetic field the two pulses would be exactly half a turn apart. Here they are separated by 165.4 degrees instead of 180, which the authors read as a sign that the field is lopsided or more complex than a simple bar magnet.

"The observed deviation is an important clue pointing to a more complicated magnetic field structure." (Scott Hyman, Sweet Briar College, via ScienceAlert)

White dwarf or magnetar?

The study does not settle what the object is. A neutron star, possibly a highly magnetic one called a magnetar, is an obvious candidate, but a 62-minute spin is far too slow for the way normal pulsars are thought to produce radio waves. A magnetic white dwarf, perhaps paired with a companion star, is the other main option, but the authors say they know of no white dwarf system that falls completely silent like this. Their analysis shows the emission could work in either case.

There are other gaps. The team could not measure a reliable distance; a rough estimate puts it nearly 18,000 light-years away, which they say should be treated with caution. No X-ray source was found, and a faint star near the position may be a chance alignment in a crowded part of the sky. The object could be emitting pulses too faint for past data to catch, so the researchers plan to keep watching in case it switches back on.
