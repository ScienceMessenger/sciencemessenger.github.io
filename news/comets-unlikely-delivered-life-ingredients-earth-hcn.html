Comets are packed with life's raw ingredients, but on Earth they almost always crashed too hard to deliver them

Cambridge simulations of early Earth's comet bombardment suggest hydrogen cyanide rarely survived the impact in useful amounts. Mars, with thinner air and slower impacts, may have had better odds.

By Farlight Staff · October 28, 2026 · Institute of Astronomy, University of Cambridge, UK · 4 min read

Comets are full of organic molecules, which has made them a favourite suspect for supplying the chemistry that eventually led to life on Earth. A new modelling study argues that the delivery would almost never have worked, because comets arrive too fast and break up or burn their cargo on the way in.

The paper was published on 28 October in Scientific Reports. Lead author Richard Anslow did the work at the University of Cambridge's Institute of Astronomy and is now at the UK Met Office, and his co-authors are Catriona McDonald, Amy Bonsor, Paul Rimmer and Auriol Rae.

Following one tough molecule

The team tracked hydrogen cyanide, a simple molecule of hydrogen, carbon and nitrogen that is common in comets and can feed reactions that build more complex organic compounds. It is also fairly hardy, so if it does not make it, more fragile molecules would fare worse.

The researchers combined a model of how short-period comets formed beyond Neptune and were later scattered inward with models of how comets break up in an atmosphere and how much hydrogen cyanide survives the impact. They then simulated Earth's early bombardment many times over, counting an impact as useful only if it left hydrogen cyanide above 10 millimoles per litre, the concentration they treat as relevant for prebiotic chemistry.

Too fast, too steep, too wet

Comets smaller than about 100 metres blew apart high in the air and scattered their molecules thinly. Bigger ones reached the ground, but at an average of about 28 kilometres per second, fast enough to destroy most of their hydrogen cyanide. Only rare slow impacts at shallow angles kept enough intact. Out of more than 100,000 comets bigger than 100 metres hitting the young Earth, the typical simulation produced no successful delivery at all.

Where a comet landed mattered too. A splashdown in the ocean dilutes the molecules at once, and early Earth may have had very little dry land. The paper estimates that any successful hit most likely came about 4.4 billion years ago, over 100 million years before the earliest indirect signs of life, and the authors say chemistry would have had to get going quickly before the molecules dispersed.

"It's kind of a romantic idea, but our results suggest it's highly unlikely." (Richard Anslow, University of Cambridge, via ScienceDaily)

Mars may have had better luck

Mars is smaller and has thinner air, so comets hit it more slowly, at about 20 kilometres per second on average, and almost all of the larger ones survive the descent. Setting aside the question of dry land, the simulations gave Mars about 744 useful impacts against about 87 for Earth and 24 for Venus. The authors stress that this does not mean life's chemistry actually started on Mars.

If not comets, then what? The researchers point to asteroid impacts, which were far more common, and to chemistry on the young Earth itself. Science News also mentions carbon-rich meteorites, volcanoes and hydrothermal vents as candidate sources.

The limits

This is a computer study built on models of comet orbits, atmospheres and impacts, and the authors do not fully rule out comet delivery. Science News notes that the impact calculations used an atmosphere like today's, and that the likely denser early atmosphere would have made survival even harder. In other planetary systems with tightly packed planets, comets might be slowed enough to deliver their cargo, which the team suggests could help future telescopes such as the proposed Habitable Worlds Observatory choose targets.
