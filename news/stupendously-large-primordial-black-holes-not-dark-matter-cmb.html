Could black holes as heavy as whole galaxies be the dark matter? The universe's oldest light says not in any big way

Physicists in Mainz and Abu Dhabi worked out the mark that giant black holes from the Big Bang would leave on the cosmic microwave background. The mark isn't there, which also weakens one explanation for Webb's surprisingly heavy early galaxies.

By Science Messenger Staff · October 26, 2026 · Mainz Institute for Theoretical Physics, Johannes Gutenberg University Mainz, Germany · 4 min read

There is roughly five times more dark matter in the universe than ordinary matter, and nobody knows what it is made of. One idea that has never quite gone away is that some of it could be black holes born in the first instants after the Big Bang. A study published on 26 October in Physical Review Letters now shuts the door on the heaviest version of that idea: black holes of at least 100 billion solar masses.

Black holes that never needed stars

These objects, called primordial black holes, are still hypothetical. If they exist, they would have formed straight out of unusually dense pockets of the infant universe rather than from dying stars, so in principle they could have almost any mass. None has been confirmed.

At the extreme end sit what researchers have nicknamed stupendously large black holes, with masses above about 10¹¹ Suns. Some would outweigh entire galaxies. They are too big to live inside galaxies, so they could never be all of the dark matter, but earlier work had left room for them to be a sizeable fraction, for masses between roughly 10¹⁴ and 10¹⁹ Suns.

A universe that would look grainy

The new paper comes from Christopher Gerlach, Yann Gouttenoire and Nicholas Leister at the Mainz Institute for Theoretical Physics and Antonio Iovino of New York University Abu Dhabi. Their starting point is simple: if dark matter came in a small number of enormous lumps scattered at random, some regions would end up with more lumps than others. At first, extra black holes in one place are balanced by a shortfall of radiation there. As the universe expanded and radiation faded, that balance broke, and the uneven spread of black holes began to tug on everything around it.

That unevenness would be stamped on the cosmic microwave background, the light released about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, whose temperature changes by only about one part in 100,000 across the sky. The team calculated the expected pattern and set it against limits from the Planck satellite's maps of that light, combined with surveys of how galaxies are spread across space. The predicted signal came out stronger than the data allow, across masses from about 10⁸ to 10¹⁹ Suns.

"So these black holes can't be a significant part of the dark matter." (Nicholas Leister, Mainz Institute for Theoretical Physics, via Space.com)

A knock for one idea about Webb's early galaxies

The James Webb Space Telescope has spotted early galaxy candidates that look surprisingly massive for their age. One proposal is that giant primordial black holes helped matter clump together faster. The authors say their limits challenge that scenario, and also strengthen the case that the gravitational-wave background seen by pulsar timing arrays is not made by these black holes merging.

What is still open

The study does not show that such objects cannot exist, only that they cannot carry much of the dark matter. It is a calculation tested against existing data, not a new observation, and it assumes every black hole has the same mass; a population with a spread of masses still needs to be checked. Upcoming microwave experiments such as LiteBIRD and the Simons Observatory should tighten the limits.

Lighter primordial black holes are a different story. According to the paper, the one mass range where they could still account for all of the dark matter is the so-called asteroid window, roughly 10⁻¹⁶ to 10⁻¹² solar masses, comparable to the mass of asteroids. The Mainz team told Space.com it is now looking at whether that window can be closed too.
