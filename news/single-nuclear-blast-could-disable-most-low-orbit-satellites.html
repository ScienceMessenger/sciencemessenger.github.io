One nuclear blast in orbit could disable almost every low-orbit satellite within a week, model suggests

A simulation of a single one-megaton detonation found that an artificial radiation belt would wear down roughly 98% of more than 13,000 satellites in seven days. The study is a preprint and has not yet been peer reviewed.

By Farlight Staff · November 12, 2026 · University of Wisconsin–Madison, United States · 4 min read

Thousands of satellites now circle close to Earth, carrying internet links, imaging, weather data and navigation support. A new modelling study asks what would happen to them if a nuclear weapon went off in their midst. Its answer: the explosion itself would do limited harm, but what it leaves behind could take out almost all of them.

The work was led by Zachary Petersen, an undergraduate at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, with co-authors Hong Qin, Sarah Thiele and Sébastien Philippe, and involved researchers at Princeton University. It was posted on the preprint server arXiv on 6 November and has not yet been through peer review.

A slow-acting radiation trap

The team simulated a one-megaton bomb detonated 500 kilometres above the South Atlantic, near a region where Earth's magnetic field is weak, known as the South Atlantic Anomaly. They used real orbital data for roughly 13,300 satellites active in low Earth orbit in January 2026.

In the model, the initial flash of radiation disabled only about 3% of them. The bigger danger came afterwards. Radioactive debris from the blast releases electrons that get caught in Earth's magnetic field, forming an artificial radiation belt. Every time a satellite passes through it, it absorbs another dose, until its electronics fail. About a quarter were out of action after one day, 78% after three days and roughly 98% after a week.

"An artificial radiation belt could turn a single nuclear detonation into a fleet-wide failure event." (the study's authors, as quoted by Phys.org)

Tougher hardware only slowed things down. When the team tripled how much radiation satellites could tolerate, about 97% were still disabled within two weeks, according to the preprint. ScienceAlert reports that a smaller, 100-kiloton blast in the model knocked out about 99% over roughly a year.

Dead satellites keep causing trouble

Disabled satellites cannot steer out of each other's way. The authors estimate around 20 collisions in the first two years, and ScienceAlert reports some 3,900 new debris fragments after a decade. Replacement satellites would fly straight into the lingering radiation, so restoring services could take years.

This is not purely hypothetical. In 1962 the United States tested a 1.4-megaton warhead, Starfish Prime, about 400 kilometres above the Pacific, and it disabled several of the few satellites then in orbit. The following year the Partial Test Ban Treaty banned nuclear tests in space.

The numbers come from a computer model, and they depend on its assumptions about how much radiation satellites can withstand and how the trapped particles behave. They are best read as an estimate of scale, not a forecast.
