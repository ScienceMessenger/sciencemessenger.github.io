Australia's bogong moths carry a magnetic compass as well as a star compass

Lab experiments show the night-flying moths can steer by the angle of Earth's magnetic field, a trick also used by migrating birds, and that it only works when blue light is present.

By Science Messenger Staff · November 18, 2026 · Lund University, Sweden · 3 min read

Every year, huge numbers of bogong moths fly by night across south-eastern Australia to cool caves in the Snowy Mountains, a journey that the research team puts at roughly 1,000 kilometres. In 2025 the same group showed that the moths can hold their course using the stars of the Milky Way. A new study published in Nature on 18 November reports that they also carry a second, independent compass based on Earth's magnetic field.

The work was led by Hui Chen and Eric Warrant at Lund University in Sweden, with colleagues at Carl von Ossietzky University Oldenburg in Germany, the Australian National University and the University of South Australia.

How the experiments worked

The researchers caught migrating moths with a light trap and tested them in a purpose-built laboratory near Adaminaby in south-eastern Australia, made entirely of non-magnetic materials. Each moth was tethered in a flight arena surrounded by magnetic coils, which let the team change the magnetic field while recording which way the insect tried to fly.

When the field was reversed, the moths turned to match. The key cue was the inclination angle, the angle at which magnetic field lines meet the ground, which grows steeper towards the poles. Birds and sea turtles are known to use the same cue. Filtering out violet and blue light left the moths disoriented, which fits a leading theory that magnetic sensing in animals relies on light-sensitive chemical reactions, possibly involving proteins called cryptochromes.

"It is remarkable that two such different groups of animals use the same basic principle to detect Earth's magnetic field." (Eric Warrant, Lund University)

Two compasses are better than one

The authors suggest that having both a star compass and a magnetic compass could help the moths keep heading the right way when one cue fails, for example under cloudy skies. Insects and birds last shared a common ancestor around 700 million years ago, so the finding hints that similar biological solutions to navigation have evolved, or been kept, in very different lineages.

Ken Lohmann, a sea turtle navigation researcher at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, described the result to National Geographic as fascinating.

The study has limits. The tests were done on tethered moths in a laboratory rather than in free flight over long distances, and the magnetic sensors themselves have not been found. The cryptochrome link is inferred from the light experiments rather than shown directly. The bogong moth is listed as endangered, and researchers hope better knowledge of how it migrates will help protect it.
