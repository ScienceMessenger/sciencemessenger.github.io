After eight years and nine planetary flybys, BepiColombo has reached Mercury

The European-Japanese mission is beginning its capture into orbit around the smallest planet, which only one spacecraft has orbited before. Its X-ray telescope has already passed its first test on a distant nebula.

By Farlight Staff · November 21, 2026 · European Space Agency (ESOC), Germany · 4 min read

Getting into orbit around Mercury is harder than it sounds. The planet sits deep in the Sun's gravity well, so a spacecraft falling toward it picks up enormous speed that has to be shed before it can stay. BepiColombo, a joint mission of the European Space Agency (ESA) and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA), has spent eight years doing exactly that, and 21 November 2026 is the date set for its capture by Mercury's gravity.

Only one spacecraft has orbited Mercury before: NASA's MESSENGER, from 2011 to 2015. BepiColombo will be the first mission to put two orbiters around the planet at the same time.

The long way round

BepiColombo launched in October 2018 as a stack of three craft: ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter (MPO), JAXA's Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter, known as Mio, and a propulsion stage called the Mercury Transfer Module. To lose speed it swung past Earth once, Venus twice and Mercury six times.

The trip did not go to plan. In 2024 a power fault stopped the transfer module's electric thrusters from running at full strength, and the team had to redesign the route. That pushed arrival back from December 2025 to November 2026 and meant flying just 165 kilometres above Mercury on the fourth flyby, closer than the 200 kilometres originally treated as safe. The transfer module was released on 3 September 2026, its job done.

"For the first time, we'll have a single mission with two orbiters going around the same planet." (Geraint Jones, ESA BepiColombo project scientist, via IFLScience)

What comes next

Capture is only the start. ESA has planned a series of engine burns running into early 2027 to shape the orbits. MPO and Mio are due to separate on 9 and 10 December. Mio will settle into a stretched orbit lasting about nine hours to study Mercury's magnetic field and its thin wisp of an atmosphere, while MPO lowers itself into a tighter loop of about two and a half hours to map the surface. Science operations are scheduled to begin in April 2027.

There is plenty to explain. Mercury's iron core reaches about 85 per cent of the way to the surface, a far bigger share than Earth's. The planet has a global magnetic field, though a weak one, and it is slowly shrinking as it cools. Its permanently shadowed polar craters appear to hold ice, and its surface is pocked with odd shallow pits called hollows that MESSENGER found but could not fully explain.

One instrument is already showing what it can do. The Mercury Imaging X-ray Spectrometer (MIXS), built by a consortium led by the University of Leicester in the UK, is the first imaging X-ray telescope sent on a planetary mission. It will map which chemical elements make up Mercury's rocks by catching the X-rays they give off when the Sun's X-rays strike them. In a 39-hour calibration run on 11 and 12 September it observed the Crab Nebula, about 6,500 light-years away, and produced images slightly sharper than in its 2015 laboratory tests, with no stray light and no sign of radiation damage to its detector.

"MIXS is generally performing fantastically well in space!" (Emma Bunce, MIXS principal investigator, University of Leicester, via Phys.org)

Some instrument effects are still being studied, and all the dates after capture are plans that could shift. The first real science from Mercury's orbit is still months away.
