Fossil fuel CO2 emissions are set to dip slightly in 2026, but total emissions still hit a record

The annual Global Carbon Budget projects a small fall in emissions from coal, oil and gas this year, mostly because oil use dropped. Fires in south-east Asia mean total emissions are still the highest on record.

By Farlight Staff · November 10, 2026 · University of Exeter, United Kingdom · 4 min read

Carbon dioxide released by burning fossil fuels and making cement is projected to fall by about 0.4% in 2026, to 38.2 billion tonnes. That is the main finding of the Global Carbon Budget 2026, the 21st yearly assessment by the Global Carbon Project, an international team of more than 140 scientists. The University of Exeter leads it, working with the University of East Anglia, CICERO in Norway, LMU Munich, the Alfred Wegener Institute and many other institutions.

The team describes the figure as a preliminary projection with a wide range. Its estimate runs from a fall of 1.5% to a rise of 0.6%, so the authors say a small increase cannot be ruled out.

Oil drives the dip

Most of the decline comes from oil, where emissions are projected to fall by 1.9%. The researchers link this mainly to the energy shock from the conflict involving Iran, which cut oil use. Gas emissions are projected to fall by 0.3%, while coal emissions are expected to rise by 0.6%.

Country by country, fossil emissions are projected to fall in China (down 0.9%), the United States (down 1.3%), the European Union (down 1.5%) and Japan (down 1%). India is the main exception, with emissions projected to grow by 4.4%. China still produces about 32% of global fossil emissions.

Land use pushes the total to a record

Emissions from land-use change, such as deforestation, are projected to rise to 5.1 billion tonnes this year, up from 4.4 billion in 2025. The team attributes much of the jump to peat fires and forest degradation in south-east Asia during drier conditions linked to the onset of El Niño. Adding land use to fossil emissions gives a projected record total of 43.3 billion tonnes, slightly above the 42.8 billion tonnes of 2025.

The concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere is projected to average 427.7 parts per million in 2026, about 54% above pre-industrial levels. The researchers estimate that 150 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide can still be emitted for a 50% chance of limiting warming to 1.5C, which is less than four years of emissions at the current rate.

"A drop in emissions in one year is not a peak unless it is driven by structural change." (Glen Peters, research professor, CICERO)

A companion study by the same group in Nature Climate Change estimates that solar power, wind power and electric vehicles offset roughly two thirds of the extra emissions that rising energy demand would otherwise have caused this year. Its authors argue that a lasting peak in global emissions could come within a few years if clean technology keeps spreading faster, but they stress that this year's dip is not yet evidence of such a peak.

There are important limits. The full Global Carbon Budget is published as a preprint in Earth System Science Data and has not yet finished peer review. Estimates for land-use emissions carry much larger uncertainties than those for fossil fuels, and the effect of the developing El Niño on forests and soils is expected to show up mostly in 2027.
